Karl's Catapult · Kalman filter workshop

State estimation and the Kalman filter · Hands-on

Help Karl the Little take the castle

Karl the Little wants to conquer a castle with his catapult. He knows the physics of a throw — but his model alone cannot tell him where the stones really are, and his knights' observations are anything but precise. Your job: combine both with a Kalman filter.

The model

Neglecting air drag, a stone thrown from the origin with speed v0 at angle θ0 follows

sx(t)=v0⁢cos⁡θ0⋅t sy(t)=v0⁢sin⁡θ0⋅t−12gt2

with gravity g = 9.81 m/s². The velocity is the time derivative: vx = v0 cos θ0 stays constant, vy = v0 sin θ0 − g t decreases.

For the filter we use the discrete-time state-space form from the lecture, with state x = [sx, sy, vx, vy]T and time step Δt:

xk+1=Axk+Bu+wk,wk∼N(0,Q) zk=Hxk+vk,vk∼N(0,R)

A constant-velocity motion model, gravity as a known (systematic) disturbance u, and an observation that measures only the position:

A= [10Δt0010Δt00100001] , Bu= [000−gΔt] , H= [10000100]
Kalman filter equations (from the lecture)

Predict — what should have happened

x^k−=Ax^k−1+Bu Pk−=APk−1AT+Q

Update — what we saw happen

Kk=Pk−HT(HPk−HT+R)−1 x^k=x^k−+Kk(zk−Hx^k−) Pk=(I−KkH)Pk−
Task 1

The true trajectory and the optimal angle

Before any stone flies, Karl wants to know how his catapult should behave. Compute the true trajectory of a stone thrown at 14 m/s and find the angle that throws it the furthest.

  1. Implement sx(t) and sy(t) to compute the true trajectory sTrue.
  2. Find the launch angle theta that maximises the throwing distance.
  3. Compute the time of landing tf (the stone is back on the ground) and use it as the end of the time vector.
Hint

Solve sy(t) = 0 for t > 0 to get the landing time. For the angle you can either reason analytically or loop over many angles, e.g. linspace(deg2rad(1), deg2rad(89), 500), and keep the one with the largest distance (argmax). plotLines lets you plot distance over angle.

Task 2

The knights' observations

Karl's knights are supposed to track every stone through the air. Unfortunately, they found their goblets more interesting than their duties — their measurements are shaky. Simulate what they observe and how wrong it is.

  1. Create the observation times tObs for a measurement rate of 10 Hz.
  2. Generate the observations sObs: the true position plus random noise (randn(0, sigma)).
  3. Compute the error of each observation (its distance to the true position) and the mean error meanErrObs.
Hint

dist(a, b) returns the Euclidean distance between two points and mean(array) the average. sTrueAtObs[k] is the true position at time tObs[k].

Task 3

Kalman filter: predict and update

Karl decides to combine what should happen (his model) with what his knights saw happen (the observations). Time for a Kalman filter.

  1. Implement the prediction step. Run the filter with prediction only (x = xPred; P = PPred;) and look at the error. Why does the open-loop prediction drift away from the true trajectory?
  2. Implement the update step and compute the mean error meanErrEst.
  3. Tune the noise covariances Q and R until the filter beats the raw observations. What do large and small values mean?
Hint

Translate the equations from the model section one by one. Matrix products are written mul(A, B, C), sums add(A, B), differences sub(A, B), transposes T(A) and inverses inv(A). For example A P AT + Q becomes add(mul(A, P, T(A)), Q). A good starting point for R is the variance of the knights' noise, sigma ** 2.

Task 4

Estimating what nobody measured

The knights only report positions — yet the filter state also contains the velocity. How good is the filter at estimating something that is never measured?

  1. Compute the true velocity vTrue at every estimation time.
  2. Compute the velocity error errVel and its mean meanErrVel.
  3. Compare estimated and true velocity in both directions. Where is the estimate worst, and why?
Bonus

Behind the castle wall

The castle wall blocks the knights' view: they can only see the first half of each flight. Can the filter still tell Karl where the stone lands?

  1. Change your filter so that it only uses observations while t ≤ ½ tf.
  2. Afterwards, keep predicting (no update) until the stone lands.
  3. Plot the estimate and the error. How does the uncertainty P evolve without observations? How far off is the predicted landing point?
More experiments from the lecture
  • Wrong initial value: start the filter with a wrong x0 (e.g. zero velocity) and a large P0. How fast does it converge?
  • Strong winds high up: add a horizontal disturbance to the true trajectory above a certain height. Which of Q or R helps the filter cope?
  • Model mismatch: set u to zero (Karl forgot gravity). What happens with small and large Q?

Toolbox

Matrices are arrays of rows: [[1, 2], [3, 4]]. Column vectors are n×1 matrices made with col([…]). Points are plain arrays [x, y].

Matrices

zeros(r, c), ones(r, c)matrix of zeros / ones
diag([a, b, …])diagonal matrix
col([a, b, …])column vector
mul(A, B, …)matrix product A·B·… (numbers act as scalars)

T(A)transpose Aᵀ
