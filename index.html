The math you need

The slides ended with the open-loop observer. This section takes it from there: how the catapult becomes a discrete-time model, why the open-loop observer is not enough, and how the Kalman filter fixes it. Each part says which task needs it.

1 The physics of a throw · Task 1

Neglecting air drag, a stone thrown from the origin with speed v0 at angle θ0 follows

sx(t)=v0⁢cos⁡θ0⋅t sy(t)=v0⁢sin⁡θ0⋅t−12gt2

with gravity g = 9.81 m/s². The velocity is the time derivative of the position (needed again in Task 4):

vx(t)=v0⁢cos⁡θ0 , vy(t)=v0⁢sin⁡θ0−gt

Landing time and distance. The stone is back on the ground when sy(t) = 0. Factor out t:

t(v0sin⁡θ0−12gt)=0 ⇒ tf=2v0sin⁡θ0g , d(θ0)=sx(tf)=v02⋅2sin⁡θ0cos⁡θ0g=v02sin⁡(2θ0)g

The distance is largest where sin(2θ0) is largest. Which angle is that?

2 From continuous to discrete time · Tasks 3–5

A filter runs on a computer and receives measurements at discrete times tk = k Δt. So we first collect everything we need to know about the stone in a state vector and write the physics as a first-order differential equation:

x= [sxsyvxvy] , x˙= [vxvy0−g] = [0010000100000000]⏟F x+ [000−g]⏟b

For a small time step the derivative is approximately constant, so we step forward with the Euler method, xk+1 ≈ xk + Δt · ẋk:

xk+1≈ (I+ΔtF)⏟A xk+ Δtb⏟Bu A= [10Δt0010Δt00100001] , B= [000000000000000Δt] , u= [000−g]

Read row by row: new position = old position + velocity · Δt, and new vy = old vy − g Δt. This is a constant-velocity model with gravity as a known input u.

Keep in mind: the model is an approximation. The exact solution has sy,k+1 = sy,k + vy,k Δt − ½ g Δt². Euler drops this last term in every step. Small, but it adds up. You will meet it again in Task 3.

Process noise. Every model is wrong a little: discretisation errors, wind, a stone that is not a perfect point. We describe all of it as a random acceleration ak ~ 𝒩(0, σa²) acting during one step. A constant acceleration changes the position by ½ a Δt² and the velocity by a Δt:

xk+1=Axk+Bu+Gak⏟wk , G= [12Δt212Δt2ΔtΔt] , wk∼N(0,Q) , Q=σa2GGT

In the code, Q = scale(mul(G, T(G)), 50) means σa² = 50 (m/s²)².

Measurement model. The knights report only the position, disturbed by independent noise with standard deviation σ in each direction:

zk=Hxk+vk , H= [10000100] , vk∼N(0,R) , R= [σ200σ2]

3 Measuring the error · Tasks 2–4

To judge any estimate ŝk (an observation or a filter output) we compare it with the true position sk at the same time. The error is the Euclidean distance, averaged over all N time steps:

ek=‖s^k−sk‖= (s^x,k−sx,k)2+(s^y,k−sy,k)2 , e¯=1N∑k=1Nek

The same formula works for the velocity in Task 4. A sanity check for Task 2: for 2-D Gaussian noise with standard deviation σ per axis, the expected distance is σ√(π/2) ≈ 1.25 σ.

4 Recap: the open-loop observer · Task 3, step 1

The open-loop observer from the slides simply runs the model forward from an initial guess x̂0. The noise is unknown, so we use its mean, zero:

x^k=Ax^k−1+Bu

Subtracting this from the true system gives the estimation error ek = xk − x̂k:

ek=Aek−1+wk−1

Nothing in this equation pulls the error back to zero. A wrong start and every bit of model error are carried along forever, and new errors are added in each step. We can track how large the error is with its covariance Pk = E[ekekT]. Since wk−1 is independent of ek−1:

Pk=APk−1AT+Q

The uncertainty only grows. The diagonal of P holds the variances of sx, sy, vx, vy; for example ±2√P11 is a 95 % band for sx. These two equations are exactly the prediction step of the Kalman filter.

5 Closing the loop: the Kalman filter · Tasks 3–5

To stop the drift we feed back the measurement. After predicting x̂k− (the superscript “−” means “before the measurement”), we compare what we expect to see, H x̂k−, with what we do see, zk, and correct by a gain Kk:

x^k=x^k−+Kk(zk−Hx^k−)⏟innovation

The question is how much to trust the measurement, i.e. how to choose Kk. The Kalman filter picks the gain that makes the variance of the new estimate as small as possible.

The idea in one dimension. Say the prediction is x̂− with variance P− and a sensor measures z = x + v with variance R. We combine both as x̂ = (1 − K) x̂− + K z. Prediction error and measurement noise are independent, so

P(K)=(1−K)2P−+K2R ⇒dP/dK=0 K=P−P−+R , P=(1−K)P−=P−RP−+R
  • The gain is always between 0 and 1: the estimate lies between prediction and measurement.
  • Perfect sensor (R → 0): K → 1, the filter follows the measurement. Perfect model (P− → 0): K → 0, the measurement is ignored.
  • The new variance is smaller than both P− and R: fusing two uncertain sources beats each source alone.

The same with matrices. With a state vector and a measurement matrix H, the division becomes a matrix inverse. H P−HT is the predicted uncertainty in measurement space, so S = H P−HT + R plays the role of P− + R. Together with the prediction from section 4, this gives the complete algorithm:

Initialise with a guess x̂0 and its uncertainty P0. Then for k = 1, 2, …

Predict — what should have happened

x^k−=Ax^k−1+Bu Pk−=APk−1AT+Q

Update — what we saw happen

Kk=Pk−HT(HPk−HT+R)−1 x^k=x^k−+Kk(zk−Hx^k−) Pk=(I−KkH)Pk−

Sizes in our example: x̂ 4×1, P, A, Q 4×4, H 2×4, R 2×2, z 2×1, K 4×2.

6 What the knobs do · Tasks 3–5

Tuning Q and R (Task 3). Only the ratio of model trust to sensor trust matters for the gain:

  • Q large: “my model is bad”. P− grows quickly, K is large, the estimate follows the noisy observations.
  • R large: “my knights are bad”. K is small, the estimate follows the model and becomes smooth, but it reacts slowly and can drift.
  • A good starting point for R is the true sensor variance, σ2 on the diagonal. Q has to cover every model error, including the term Euler neglects.

Estimating what nobody measured (Task 4). H picks out only the position, yet the velocity is corrected too. The reason is in P. Look at one axis with state [s, v]: starting from an uncorrelated P = diag(ps, pv), one prediction step gives (ignoring Q)

P−= [1Δt01] [ps00pv] [10Δt1] = [ps+Δt2pvΔtpvΔtpvpv] ⇒ K=1P11−+R [P11−Δtpv]

Because a velocity error turns into a position error one step later, the model creates a correlation between the two (the off-diagonal entries). The second row of K is therefore not zero: a surprising position also corrects the velocity. The velocity is inferred from how positions change over time, so it needs a few steps to settle.

No observations (Bonus). Without a measurement there is nothing to update, which is the same as Kk = 0: the filter falls back to the open-loop observer, x̂k = x̂k− and Pk = Pk−, and the uncertainty grows again. The stone lands between the two prediction steps where ŝy changes sign; linear interpolation between them gives

t*=tk+s^y,ks^y,k−s^y,k+1Δt , s^x*≈s^x,k+v^x,k(t*−tk)±2P11
Task 1

The true trajectory and the optimal angle

Before any stone flies, Karl wants to know how his catapult should behave. Compute the true trajectory of a stone thrown at 14 m/s and find the angle that throws it the furthest.

  1. Implement sx(t) and sy(t) to compute the true trajectory sTrue.
  2. Find the launch angle theta that maximises the throwing distance.
  3. Compute the time of landing tf (the stone is back on the ground) and use it as the end of the time vector.
Hint

Solve sy(t) = 0 for t > 0 to get the landing time. For the angle you can either reason analytically or loop over many angles, e.g. linspace(deg2rad(1), deg2rad(89), 500), and keep the one with the largest distance (argmax). plotLines lets you plot distance over angle.

Task 2

The knights' observations

Karl's knights are supposed to track every stone through the air. Unfortunately, they found their goblets more interesting than their duties — their measurements are shaky. Simulate what they observe and how wrong it is.

  1. Create the observation times tObs for a measurement rate of 10 Hz.
  2. Generate the observations sObs: the true position plus random noise (randn(0, sigma)).
  3. Compute the error of each observation (its distance to the true position) and the mean error meanErrObs.
Hint

dist(a, b) returns the Euclidean distance between two points and mean(array) the average. sTrueAtObs[k] is the true position at time tObs[k].

Task 3

Kalman filter: predict and update

Karl decides to combine what should happen (his model) with what his knights saw happen (the observations). Time for a Kalman filter.

  1. Implement the prediction step. Run the filter with prediction only (x = xPred; P = PPred;) and look at the error. Why does the open-loop prediction drift away from the true trajectory?
  2. Implement the update step and compute the mean error meanErrEst.
  3. Tune the noise covariances Q and R until the filter beats the raw observations. What do large and small values mean?
Hint

Translate the equations from section 5 of the math one by one. Matrix products are written mul(A, B, C), sums add(A, B), differences sub(A, B), transposes T(A) and inverses inv(A). For example A P AT + Q becomes add(mul(A, P, T(A)), Q). A good starting point for R is the variance of the knights' noise, sigma ** 2.

Task 4

Estimating what nobody measured

The knights only report positions — yet the filter state also contains the velocity. How good is the filter at estimating something that is never measured?

  1. Compute the true velocity vTrue at every estimation time.
  2. Compute the velocity error errVel and its mean meanErrVel.
  3. Compare estimated and true velocity in both directions. Where is the estimate worst, and why?
Bonus

Behind the castle wall

The castle wall blocks the knights' view: they can only see the first half of each flight. Can the filter still tell Karl where the stone lands?

  1. Change your filter so that it only uses observations while t ≤ ½ tf.
  2. Afterwards, keep predicting (no update) until the stone lands.
  3. Plot the estimate and the error. How does the uncertainty P evolve without observations? How far off is the predicted landing point?
More experiments
  • Wrong initial value: start the filter with a wrong x0 (e.g. zero velocity) and a large P0. How fast does it converge?
  • Strong winds high up: add a horizontal disturbance to the true trajectory above a certain height. Which of Q or R helps the filter cope?
  • Model mismatch: set u to zero (Karl forgot gravity). What happens with small and large Q?
